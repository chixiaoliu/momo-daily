The Vinegar Festival醋节

A small town fights to save its vinegar festival and its heritage by staging a play based on an ancient book.

Elena Rossi taught literature at the small school in Marrow Bay. The town sat on a narrow peninsula, and its main product was vinegar. Every year, the town held a festival to announce the new vintage. This year, however, a recession had cut the budget. The town council had to veto the parade and the music band. Elena thought this was a scandal. The festival was the sole event that brought visitors to the town.

Elena went to see Mayor Chen. She made a plea for the festival. The mayor was not a bad man, but he was not willing to spend money. "A compromise is possible," he said. "We can hold a small event in the school canteen." Elena did not moan or weep. She simply asked for a small surplus of vinegar and a few crates of clay cups. The mayor agreed, but the extent of his help was small.

Elena walked home through the dairy fields. The terrain was flat near the sea, then rose into low hills. She saw a beetle on a stone and a mosquito near her arm. She did not slap them. She was thinking about the festival. Her niece, Mia, was visiting from the city. Mia studied biology and was full of ideas. "You need a feat," Mia said. "Something people will talk about."

The next morning, Elena found a letter in her mailbox. It was from an old woman named Rosa. Rosa wrote that she had a copy of an ancient book. The book was a work of fiction, but it described a vinegar festival from 200 years ago. "Come to the gaol," Rosa wrote. "I dwell in the old building near the harbour." Elena was intrigued. She took her cart and went to see Rosa.

Rosa's room was small but warm. An old oven stood in the corner. Rosa gave Elena the book. It was a story about a queen on a throne. The queen had a loyal martyr who made vinegar for the poor. The book had a strange pronoun on every page: "we." Elena read for an hour. The story was not trivial. It had a deep connotation of hope. "This could revive our festival," Elena said.

Elena and Mia made a plan. They would stage a short play based on the book. They would use the old vinegar recipe in the story. The recipe needed a quart of spring water and a lump of brown sugar. It also needed a special fiber from a local plant. Mia said, "The organism that makes vinegar is a kind of bacteria. It needs warm air and time." Elena nodded. "We have time," she said.

The play needed actors. Elena asked her students. A boy named Tom would play the martyr. A girl named Lily would play the queen. They practised in the canteen after school. Tom had to kneel and weep. Lily had to sit on a wooden chair that looked like a throne. Elena had to scold them sometimes, but they worked hard. The play began to take form.

One afternoon, a man from the city came to the school. His name was Mr. Hale. He worked for a large food company. He wanted to buy the town's vinegar recipe. He offered a lot of money. The mayor was interested. "This could end our recession," he said. But Mr. Hale wanted a franchise. He would control the production. Elena was worried. She did not want the town to lose its independence.

Elena called a town meeting. She made a plea to the people. "We must not sell our sole recipe," she said. "It is intrinsic to who we are." A farmer stood up. "But we need money," he said. The room was full of doubt. Then Rosa stood up. She was old and had to use a ladder to reach the microphone. "I was a child during the famine," she said. "The vinegar from that recipe saved my family."

The room went quiet. Rosa's words were humane and simple. She did not use big words. She did not inflict blame on anyone. She just told her story. People began to clap. A man in the back began to weep. The mayor looked at the floor. "I will veto Mr. Hale's offer," he said. "We will keep our recipe." The people cheered. Elena felt a quick quake in her chest. It was hope.

The festival day arrived. The weather was brisk. A cold wind came from the sea. Elena wore a warm scarf around her thigh. She had hurt her leg the day before, but she did not let it spoil the day. The canteen was full. A band played outside. The players wore bright clothes. A clock on the wall moved clockwise, and the play began. Tom and Lily were wonderful. The audience was silent.

After the play, Elena served the vinegar. She had made it with the old recipe. People drank from clay cups. A woman said, "This tastes like my grandmother's vinegar." Elena smiled. She had also made bacon sandwiches and a large cake from the oven. The dairy had given her fresh milk. The festival was small, but it was full of joy. No one talked about the recession.

Later, a reporter came to the festival. She wrote a story for a city newspaper. The story was about a small town that saved its vinegar. The story put Marrow Bay in the limelight. Tourists began to come. Mr. Hale's company withdrew its offer. The mayor announced a new budget for the festival. Elena received a letter from the Ministry of Culture. It said, "Your festival is now part of our national heritage."

Elena sat in her lounge that evening. She looked at the old book from Rosa. She thought about the queen and the martyr. She thought about the pronoun "we." She thought about the strange journey of the vinegar. She had to ascertain one thing: the town had evolved. It had not abandoned its past. It had found a new way. She closed the book and felt a deep peace.

The next morning, Elena found a letter from the town council. It said the festival would now follow a new format, with a parade and a music band. Furthermore, the council had passed legislation to protect the town's vinegar recipe. "We will not let anyone kidnap our heritage," the mayor wrote. Elena smiled. She knew the town would wield its independence wisely.

At the festival, Elena added new events. There was a badminton tournament and a bout of tug-of-war. A local artist painted a mural on the exterior wall of the canteen. "It's a sensible way to attract visitors," Mia said. The mural showed the town's longitude and latitude, and a plough in a field. "It will suffice to make us famous," Elena laughed.

Rosa told stories about the indigenous people who first made vinegar. "They would flee from storms and bury their vinegar jars to keep them safe," she said. "Notwithstanding the torture of hunger, they never gave up." Elena felt a stab of pride. "We must remember their vogue for simple living," she said. "An ounce of tradition is worth a pound of profit."

The festival was a success. The town became autonomous in its decisions. People came from far away to taste the vinegar. Elena knew that the town would never resemble the big city, and that was fine. The old book had taught them to strip away what was not needed and keep what is true.

The cumulative effect of these small efforts was clear: the festival grew a little each year. Elena felt a patriotic pride in her town's quiet strength.
