The Bee Man of Coral Bay珊瑚湾的养蜂人

A retired headmaster inherits a mysterious diary and turns a family scandal into a bee education center.

Arthur Pemberton was a well-off retired headmaster in the small town of Coral Bay. He had a sole passion: his bees. Every morning he would walk down to the hive at the bottom of his garden, wearing his old striped sock and a dim lantern. He would clap his hands softly to warn the insects, then lift the lid. The bees were gentle, and their busy motion around the frames always made him smile. It was a small, intrinsic pleasure, and it kept him from feeling ail.

Arthur's garden was a mess of wildflowers and old crates. He had let it grow that way on purpose, because the bees loved the tangle of plants. A narrow basin collected rainwater near the fence, and a stone pyramid stood in one corner, covered in moss. Arthur would often sit there with a pint of tea, watching the bees return with their legs dusted yellow. He believed this little patch of terrain held a kind of magic, an illusion of a perfect world.

One Tuesday, a letter arrived from a lawyer in the city. It said that a distant relative had died and left Arthur a piece of land on the peninsula. The news came as a shock. Arthur had never met this relative, an imperial aristocrat who had lived abroad. The letter mentioned a memorial fund and a small auction of the dead man's things. Arthur felt a strange nerve in his stomach. He had no claim to such wealth, he thought, and yet the letter was genuine.

Arthur decided to go to the auction. He took the train along the coast, watching the coral-colored cliffs slide past. The auction house was a tall tower of red brick, with a brass sign above the door. Inside, the room was full of well-off people holding glasses of wine. Arthur felt out of place in his crude wool coat. He found a seat at the back and tried to be silent. A servant brought him a glass of water and a small plate of refreshments.

The auction began with a brisk series of small items: old books, a brass compass, a crate of china. Arthur watched the numbers exceed his expectations. Then the auctioneer brought out a wooden box. "A collection of letters and a diary," he said. "From the estate of the late Mr. Edgar Pemberton." Arthur's heart gave a sting. Edgar was the name of his own father, who had left Coral Bay when Arthur was a boy and never returned.

Arthur raised his hand and made a bid. The price rose, but he was determined. He felt a sudden bout of courage, and he would not let the box go. When the hammer fell, he had won. He paid and took the box outside. His hands were not quite steady as he opened it on a bench. The diary was full of small, neat writing. The first page read: "For my son, Arthur, if he ever comes looking."

Back home, Arthur read the diary by the dim light of his lamp. Edgar had been a missionary in South America. He wrote about the heat, the insects, and the strange customs of the people. He wrote about a woman he loved and a child he had left behind. Arthur felt the old anger rise. His father had abandoned them. But as he read on, he began to see a different story, one full of torture and difficult choices.

The diary described a corrupt official who had tried to steal Edgar's land. Edgar had fled with the child, but the journey went wrong. He wrote of a terrible night on a mountain path, when he had to choose between saving the child and saving himself. The child was lost. Edgar wrote that he had never forgiven himself. He had spent years trying to justify his actions, but the guilt would not leave him.

Arthur put the diary down. The bees were humming outside in the dark. He thought about his father's life, so far from this quiet garden. He had always imagined Edgar as a cold man, but the diary showed a man who was naive and frightened. Arthur felt a strange sense of favor toward him now. He walked to the hive and listened to the steady sound. The bees did not care about the past. They only worked for the future.

The next morning, Arthur went to see his old friend Mrs. Bell, who ran the local shop. She was a jolly woman with a sharp eye for scandal. Arthur told her about the diary and the land on the peninsula. Mrs. Bell listened with great interest. "You must go and see the land," she said. "It might be worth a fortune." Arthur shook his head. He was not sure he wanted to be rich. He only wanted to understand.

Arthur took a bus to the peninsula. The land was wild and beautiful, with a view of the sea. There was an old stone house on the hill, half fallen down. A small oven stood in the yard, and a broken throne of white stone. Arthur walked around the land, feeling the grass under his feet. He found a marker with the name Pemberton on it. He stood there for a long time, thinking about his father and the child who was lost.

On the way back, Arthur stopped at a small café. He ordered a pint of beer and a plate of fish. A group of students at the next table were talking about psychiatry and the human mind. One of them said that memory was a kind of story we tell ourselves. Arthur thought about that. His father had told himself a story too, one that had kept him away from his family. Arthur wondered if he could tell a different story now.

That evening, Arthur made a decision. He would not sell the land. Instead, he would use it to build a small center for the study of bees. He would invite schoolchildren to visit and learn about these tiny insects. He would call it the Edgar Pemberton Memorial Garden. The idea felt sensible and good. He would use the money from his savings to make it happen. He would not let the past spoil the future.

Arthur wrote a letter to the lawyer and made his plan clear. He would take a long lease on the land and restore the old house. He would plant flowers and build a proper hive. He would teach the children how to care for bees and how to respect nature. The work would be hard, but he was ready. He stood in his garden one last time before bed. The bees were asleep in their hive. The moon was bright above the coral sea. Arthur felt a deep peace, and he smiled.

Months later, the center opened. Children came from all over the region to see the bees. Arthur stood by the gate and welcomed them. He felt a genuine joy as he watched them learn. He had turned a family scandal into something good. The past was still there, but it no longer ruled him. He had found a way to honor his father and to forgive him. The bees flew in and out of the hive, busy and free, and Arthur was happy.

Arthur's days became a lavish overflow of activity. He would moan about his aching back, but the work was a feat of love. He kept an encyclopedia of bee facts on a shelf, and he would sprinkle sugar water for the bees. He watched a beetle intrude on the hive and used a straw to gently guide it away. The bees would flee from the smoke when he checked the frames. He felt the almighty power of nature in every small task.

The center grew to a great magnitude. Arthur had to deduct expenses from his savings, but he didn't mind. He would slot each new donation into a special fund. The bees seemed to resemble tiny workers, each with an autonomous role. He learned to wield a smoker with skill. He would disarm visitors with his warm smile. He spoke to Congress about the importance of bees. He became the foremost expert in the region.

Arthur's medical checkups were thorough, and he stayed healthy. He would worship the quiet mornings in his garden. The ozone smell after rain was a climax to his day. He saw the axis of his life shift from loneliness to purpose. He had a heroine: his own mother, who had raised him alone. He kept a small sphere of glass on his desk, a reminder of the world's beauty. He felt a deep contentment.

Arthur also began to write about his father. He did not want the story to stagnate in old letters. He wrote an academic article about Edgar's life and the history of the land. He even found traces of cocaine in an old medicine bottle from the estate, a sign of hard times. To some extent, the writing helped him forgive. He sent the article to a journal and waited.
